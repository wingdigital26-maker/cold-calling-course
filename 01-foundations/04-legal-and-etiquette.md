# Legal Basics and Etiquette (US)

**This is general education, not legal advice.** Laws change, states differ, and penalties for getting telemarketing law wrong can be serious. Before you start calling at any real volume, spend an hour reading the current FTC and FCC guidance, and consider a quick consult with a lawyer if you plan to scale. What follows is the broad landscape as commonly understood; verify anything you rely on.

## TCPA (Telephone Consumer Protection Act)

The TCPA is the main federal law governing calls and texts. The parts most relevant to you:

- It restricts calls made with autodialers and prerecorded/artificial voice messages, especially to cell phones, without the right kind of consent. Manual, live, one-at-a-time dialing by a human is generally on much safer ground than any automated dialing or ringless voicemail scheme.
- Penalties are assessed per call and can add up fast. TCPA lawsuits are common and there are plaintiffs' firms that specialize in them.
- Practical takeaway for a beginner: dial manually, talk live, do not blast prerecorded voicemails, and do not send cold marketing texts without understanding the consent rules first. Text messages are treated much more strictly than a live manual call.

The exact definitions (what counts as an autodialer, what consent is required for what) have shifted with court decisions and FCC rules over the years, which is exactly why you should check current guidance rather than rely on a summary like this one.

## The Do-Not-Call Registry, and the B2B nuance

The national Do-Not-Call (DNC) registry, run by the FTC, lets consumers opt out of telemarketing calls.

The nuance that matters for you: **the federal DNC rules are aimed at calls to consumers. Business-to-business calls are generally treated differently and largely outside the registry's core scope.** If you are calling a roofing company's business line to sell services to the business, that is a B2B call.

But be careful with the gray areas:

- Many small home-service businesses run on the owner's **personal cell phone**. A number can be both a personal line and a business line, and personal numbers may be on the DNC registry. The B2B framing gets murkier when you are ringing someone's personal cell.
- Some states have their own telemarketing and DNC laws, and a few are stricter than federal law or sweep in more call types. State law can apply based on where the person you are calling is located, not just where you are.
- If anyone tells you to stop calling them, honor it immediately and permanently, whatever the registry status. Keep your own internal do-not-call list and actually check it before dialing.

When in doubt, act as if the stricter rule applies. The cost of skipping one prospect is one prospect. The cost of a violation can be a lot more.

## Calling hours

Federal telemarketing rules for calls to consumers set a window of **8 a.m. to 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time zone**, and some states set narrower windows. Even where a strict legal window may not apply to a pure B2B call, staying inside 8 to 9 local time is the safe, simple habit, and honestly you should be tighter than that anyway:

- For home-service businesses, roughly 7:30 to 9:00 a.m. (before they head to job sites) and 4:00 to 6:00 p.m. (back in the truck or office) tend to be when owners actually answer. Midday, they are on a roof or under a house.
- Mind time zones if you call outside your own area. "Recipient's local time" is what counts.
- Avoid major holidays. Nothing good comes from cold calling on Thanksgiving.

## Recording calls: consent basics

If you record calls (for self-review or training), consent law applies, and it is state law, so it varies:

- Some states are **one-party consent**: one participant in the call (which can be you) knowing about the recording is enough.
- Other states are **all-party consent** (often called two-party consent): everyone on the call must consent. California is the best-known example of a stricter state.
- On an interstate call, the safe practice is to follow the stricter state's rule, since it is not always clear which law a court would apply.

Simplest safe habits: either do not record, or say up front "I'm recording this call for my own training, is that alright?" and stop if they object. Check your own state's current rule and the rules of the states you call into before recording anything.

## Etiquette: the non-legal rules that keep you welcome

None of this is law. All of it protects your reputation and your results.

- **Identify yourself immediately.** Name and company in the first sentence. Hiding who you are reads as scam behavior and violates the spirit (and for consumer telemarketing, the letter) of the rules anyway.
- **Take no for an answer, once.** One thoughtful push past an initial brush-off is salesmanship. Three is harassment. If the no is clear, thank them and get off the phone.
- **"Take me off your list" means forever.** Log it, honor it, never dial it again.
- **Do not argue, ever.** If someone is rude, "No problem, have a good one" and hang up. You lose nothing; arguing loses you the next call's energy too.
- **Respect the gatekeeper.** The receptionist can block you forever or put you through next Tuesday. Be pleasant, use their name if offered, never lie about why you are calling.
- **Do not fake familiarity.** "Hey, just following up on our conversation!" when there was no conversation is a lie, and owners smell it. Honesty in the opener outperforms tricks anyway (see file 02).
- **Leave short voicemails or none.** If you leave one, keep it under 20 seconds: name, company, one-line reason, number. Never do robo or ringless voicemail drops.
- **Hang up respectfully every time.** The roofer who brushed you off in March might call you in July when the slow season hits. Every call ends with the door open or closed; keep it open.

## The short version

- Dial manually, talk live, no robocalls, no cold marketing texts.
- Treat B2B as a helpful nuance, not a magic shield, especially on personal cells.
- Call 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. recipient's local time at the absolute widest; owner-friendly hours are narrower anyway.
- Know your state's recording-consent rule before recording; when in doubt, ask permission or do not record.
- Keep and honor your own do-not-call list.
- Verify current law yourself; this file is a map, not the territory.
