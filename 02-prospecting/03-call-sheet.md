# The Call Sheet

A call sheet is the single document you run your calling day from. Everyone you plan to dial today, in order, with the outcome logged as you go. Without one, you forget who you called, you miss callbacks, and you have no idea whether you are improving. A spreadsheet or even a markdown file is fine. The tool does not matter; using it every single call does.

## Columns

Keep it to what you will actually fill in mid-call:

- **#** - row number, so you can say "mark 14 no answer" to yourself and move fast
- **Business** - name
- **Owner** - decision maker's name if known
- **Phone**
- **Hook** - your one-line researched opener note (from your prospect card)
- **Status** - see below
- **Callback** - date and time if one was agreed
- **Notes** - one line max: what happened, who you spoke to, objection raised

## Statuses

Use a fixed set. Free-text statuses turn into mush you cannot count.

- **no answer** - rang out or voicemail. Not a failure; most dials end here. They stay in rotation.
- **gatekeeper** - reached someone, but not the decision maker. Note the gatekeeper's name if you got it.
- **conversation** - spoke with the decision maker, whatever the outcome. This is the number that matters most.
- **booked** - meeting or follow-up call scheduled. The goal of the whole exercise.
- **not interested** - a clear no from the decision maker. Log it, be polite, move on. Do not re-dial these next week.
- **callback** - they asked you to call at a specific time. This is a commitment; treat it like one.

At the end of the day, counting each status tells you exactly where your funnel leaks. Lots of dials but few conversations: your list or call times need work. Lots of conversations but no bookings: your pitch needs work.

## Timeboxing a call block

Do not "make calls when you get a chance." Block it.

- Pick a fixed window, for example 9:00 to 11:00am, and protect it. Nothing else happens in that window: no email, no research, no admin.
- Research is done before the block (see [Pre-Call Research](02-pre-call-research.md)). During the block, you only dial and log.
- Dial the next row immediately after logging the last one. The 5 seconds between calls is where dread creeps in; do not give it room.
- Rough guidance, not a statistic: with research pre-done, 15 to 25 dials per hour is a normal pace for a beginner. Most will be no answers, and that is fine.
- End the block on time. Log your totals: dials, conversations, bookings. Those three numbers, tracked daily, are how you know you are getting better.

Best times to call trades are generally early morning (before crews roll out, around 7:30 to 8:30am) and late afternoon (back in the office, 4 to 5:30pm). Owners are on roofs and under sinks midday. This is field wisdom, not data; test it in your own market.

## Callback discipline

Callbacks are where beginners bleed booked deals. Rules:

1. **A callback is an appointment.** If they said Thursday at 9, you call Thursday at 9:00, not 9:40, not Friday. Being exactly on time is itself a sales pitch: it proves you do what you say.
2. **Write it down the moment they say it.** In the Callback column, with date and time, before you hang up.
3. **Start each day by pulling callbacks to the top of the sheet.** They dial first, before any fresh prospects.
4. **Open the callback by keeping your word out loud:** "Hi Terri, it's Jack, you asked me to call Thursday morning, so here I am."
5. **A vague callback ("try me some other time") is not a callback.** Log it as no answer or gatekeeper and keep them in normal rotation. Only a specific day and time earns the callback status.

## Daily reset

At the end of each day: count your statuses, carry callbacks forward, drop the not-interesteds, and load tomorrow's fresh rows. Five minutes. Tomorrow's block starts with zero setup.
