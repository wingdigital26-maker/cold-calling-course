# Solo Drills: Practice Before Real Dials

Your first 50 reps should not be on real prospects. Every drill here costs nothing and can be done alone in a room. Do them before your first dial day and keep doing them as a warmup before every call block.

## Why drill at all

On a live call you have zero spare brainpower. If you are still thinking about what to say next, you cannot listen. Drilling moves the script from "reading" to "reflex" so your attention is free for the person on the other end.

## Drill 1: Read the script aloud

Reading in your head does nothing. Your mouth needs the reps.

1. Print your script or put it on a second screen.
2. Read the opener out loud 10 times in a row.
3. Then read the full script top to bottom 5 times.
4. Now say the opener without looking. If you stumble, look, then try again.

Goal: you can deliver the opener and your core pitch without reading, in your own words, without losing the structure. Word-for-word memorization is not the goal. Knowing what each line is supposed to do is.

Rough guidance: most people need 2 to 3 days of 15-minute sessions before the opener feels automatic.

## Drill 2: Record yourself

Your phone's voice memo app is your coach.

1. Record yourself delivering the opener and pitch as if a prospect just answered.
2. Play it back. Yes, it is uncomfortable. Do it anyway.
3. Listen for three things:
   - Do you sound like you are reading? (flat, evenly paced, no natural pauses)
   - Are you rushing? (nervous callers speed up)
   - Do you sound like someone you would stay on the phone with?
4. Re-record. Compare. Repeat until the answer to the last question is yes.

Keep your first recording. In 30 days it will be the best proof of progress you have.

## Drill 3: Tonality

What you say matters less than how it lands. Three specific things to drill:

### Smile while you talk

It sounds like a gimmick. It is not. Smiling physically changes your vocal tone and the person on the other end can hear it. Record the same opener twice, once flat-faced and once smiling, and play both back. You will hear the difference. Some callers put a small mirror on their desk as a reminder.

### Pace and pausing

Nervous callers talk fast and fill every silence. Drill the opposite:

- Deliver your opener, then force a full one-second pause after your first question. Count it in your head.
- Practice slowing down on the important line (your reason for calling) and letting it breathe.
- Record yourself and check: could someone interrupt you if they wanted to? If not, you are steamrolling.

### Downward inflection on the ask

When you ask for the meeting, your voice should go down at the end of the sentence, not up. Upward inflection sounds like you are asking permission and expecting a no. Downward inflection sounds like the meeting is a normal, reasonable next step.

Drill it: say "Would Tuesday at 10 work for a quick 15 minutes?" ten times, ending the sentence lower than you started it. Record it. If it sounds like a question you are scared to ask, do ten more.

## Drill 4: Objection flashcards

You already know the objections you will hear. There is no excuse for being surprised by them.

1. Write each common objection on its own card (index cards or a notes app):
   - "Not interested."
   - "We already have someone."
   - "Now's not a good time."
   - "Just send me an email."
   - "How much does it cost?"
   - "Who is this again?"
2. Shuffle the stack.
3. Flip a card and respond out loud immediately, using the objection frameworks from the objections section of this course.
4. If you freeze or ramble, put that card back in the stack twice.

Do 10 cards a day. The goal is not a memorized comeback for each one. The goal is that hearing the objection triggers calm instead of panic.

## Daily warmup (5 minutes before every call block)

- Read the opener aloud twice.
- One recorded rep of the full pitch, quick playback.
- Five objection flashcards.
- Smile check, posture up, dial.

Do not skip the warmup because you feel ready. Athletes warm up precisely on the days that matter.
