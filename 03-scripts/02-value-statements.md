# Value Statements: Say What You Do in One Sentence

The opener buys you 30 seconds. The value statement is what you spend them on.

A value statement is one sentence that answers the only question in the prospect's head: "why should I keep listening?" If you can't say it in one breath, you don't have one yet.

## The formula

**Problem they feel + outcome they want + proof it's real.**

> "Most [TRADE] owners I talk to [PROBLEM THEY FEEL]. We [OUTCOME], and [PROOF]."

Three parts, in plain English:

1. **Problem they feel.** Not the problem you solve on paper. The one that annoys them on a Tuesday. A roofer doesn't lie awake thinking "my SEO is suboptimal." He thinks "the phone's been quiet" or "that other company gets all the storm jobs."
2. **Outcome.** What changes in their life. More calls, more booked jobs, fewer no-shows. Say the result, not the mechanism. "Get you more repair calls" beats "optimize your Google Business Profile."
3. **Proof.** Why they should believe you. A real client result, a specific thing you noticed about their business, or honest transparency ("we're new, which is why I'm offering it at X"). Never invent numbers. If you don't have results yet, don't fake them. Made-up stats get sniffed out, and one caught lie ends the relationship forever.

## Bad vs good

**Bad:**
> "We're a full-service digital marketing agency offering innovative solutions to help businesses grow their online presence and maximize ROI."

Why it's bad: it's about you, it's jargon, it describes zero problems the owner feels, and it sounds exactly like the last five agencies that called him. He's already filed you in the trash.

**Good:**
> "When people in [CITY] search for a roofer, your competitors show up before you do, which means they're getting calls that should be yours. I fix that. I'm doing it right now for a roofer one town over."

Why it's good: it names a problem he can verify himself in 10 seconds on his own phone, states an outcome in his language (calls that should be yours), and offers proof without a fake statistic.

**Bad:**
> "We leverage cutting-edge AI-driven lead generation to supercharge your pipeline."

**Good:**
> "You're probably getting most jobs from word of mouth, which is great until it's slow. I make the internet send you jobs too, so the slow months aren't so slow."

**Bad:**
> "We increase our clients' revenue by up to 300%."

Why it's bad: even if a number like that were true for one client, "up to" claims sound like a scam because they're the uniform of a scam. If you have a real result, tell the real story instead: "One client went from about 3 calls a week to 3 a day within a couple months." If you don't, skip numbers entirely.

## Adapting per niche

Same formula, different pain. You have to know what actually hurts in each trade.

**Roofer:**
The pain is storm-chaser competition and feast-or-famine work. After a hail storm, forty companies fight for the same neighborhoods.
> "After the next storm rolls through [CITY], a dozen out-of-town crews are going to flood the area with door knockers. My job is making sure when a homeowner googles instead of answering the door, they find you first."

**HVAC:**
The pain is seasonality and emergency-call competition. Summer AC failures and winter furnace failures are won by whoever the panicked homeowner finds first at 9pm.
> "When somebody's AC dies in July, they grab their phone and call the first company they see. Right now that's not you, and every one of those calls is a $[X] repair or a full replacement going to someone else. I can change who they see first."

**Plumber:**
The pain is emergency urgency plus reviews. A burst pipe means the homeowner calls whoever looks trustworthy in 30 seconds of panicked scrolling, and trustworthy means review count.
> "You've got [N] Google reviews and [COMPETITOR] up the road has [N]. When someone's got water coming through the ceiling, they call the one with more stars. I help you close that gap and catch those emergency calls."

**Landscaper:**
The pain is one-off jobs versus recurring contracts, and spring being decided in February.
> "Most landscapers get busy in spring and starve in winter. The companies that don't are the ones locked into year-round maintenance contracts, and those get won early in the year. That's the work I help you go get."

Notice what changed in each: the problem and the moment of urgency. The formula never changed.

## Rules

- One sentence to start, two max. If they want more, they'll ask. That's the goal: a value statement that earns a question.
- Say it out loud 20 times before your first call. If your mouth trips on a word, change the word.
- Use their vocabulary. Jobs, calls, crews, booked out. Not leads, funnels, conversions.
- Fill in real numbers ([N] reviews, [COMPETITOR] name) before you dial. A value statement with their actual data in it is 10x the generic version.
- No em dashes on paper and no jargon out loud. If you wouldn't say it to a guy in a truck at a gas station, don't say it on the phone.
